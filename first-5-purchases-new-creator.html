First 5 Purchases for a New Content Creator, Ranked by Impact

Last updated: August 27, 2026 · Research-based guide · 7 min read

Buy in this order: microphone, cage, tripod, light, then an ND filter or storage. The ranking is by how much each purchase changes what your audience actually experiences, not by price and not by how impressive it looks in a setup shot. Follow it and every item you own is already earning its keep before the next one arrives — which is the opposite of how most beginner kits get assembled.

The ranking at a glance

Each row is ranked by the size of the change a viewer notices, which is why the cheapest item is first and the most photogenic one is not on the list at all.

1. A wireless lavalier microphone

This is first by a wide margin and it is not close. The problem it solves is physical: your phone’s microphone is wherever your phone is, which on a piece to camera is an arm’s length away, and at that distance it records as much room as it does voice. Every hard surface in the room arrives with your voice. No amount of editing puts that back.

A lavalier moves the capsule to your collar, roughly 20cm from your mouth, and the room stops being audible. The specifications that decide which one you buy are the connector your phone uses, battery life per charge, and transmitter weight, since a heavy transmitter drags on a shirt. Start with our wireless lavalier mics under $100; if you already know you want two channels for interviews, the Rode Wireless Micro vs. DJI Mic Mini comparison covers the tier above.

2. A cage or grip

Second, because it is both a fix and a foundation. The fix is framing: two hands on a rigid rectangle removes the slow drift that reads as amateur even when the footage is otherwise sharp. The foundation is the cold shoes and 1/4″-20 threads, which is where the microphone receiver and the light will go — buy the light before the cage and you are holding a light in one hand and a phone in the other.

The one specification that decides the purchase is clamp width measured against your phone with its case on. MagSafe cages only hold properly on an iPhone. Start with the phone video cage guide, or go straight to MagSafe cages or universal Android cages if you already know which side you are on.

3. A tripod or stand

Third if you appear in your own videos, and it is the purchase most often postponed for no good reason. A tripod does not improve a shot — it makes a shot possible that was not possible before, which is a larger category of change. The moment you want a two-minute piece to camera with your hands free, nothing else in the kit substitutes.

If someone else operates the camera for you, move this to fourth and buy the light first. That is the only situation where the order changes.

4. A pocket LED light

Fourth, and it matters most if you film indoors. Interior light is dimmer and greyer than your eye reports, and a phone compensates by lifting the sensor into the range where it has to trade noise against detail. Adding a small amount of controllable light keeps it out of that range entirely.

The specification worth paying for is CRI, which describes how faithfully the light renders colour and therefore whether skin tones look healthy or slightly green. Raw brightness is much less important than beginners assume — most pocket panels are used well below full output. Our budget LED video lights guide compares them on CRI and runtime rather than headline output.

5. An ND filter, or fast storage

The fifth purchase splits by where you film, and most creators need one of these rather than both.

Shoot outdoors? Buy an ND filter. In daylight a phone keeps exposure under control by raising shutter speed, which strips motion blur and gives footage a hard, staccato look. An ND cuts the light so you can hold a shutter angle near 180 degrees and get natural motion back. See ND filters and phone lenses.

Shoot indoors and recording in high-bitrate formats? Buy fast external storage instead. The only specification that matters is sustained write speed, because that is what determines whether a recording drops frames. Our portable SSD guide covers it.

Why this order and not the popular one

The order most beginner kits get bought in — camera or phone upgrade, then a gimbal, then everything else — optimises for how the setup looks rather than how the output plays. It front-loads the two most expensive items and leaves audio, the fault viewers react to fastest, until last.

Ranking by impact inverts that. It also has a practical benefit: each item here makes the next one more useful. The microphone needs somewhere to mount, which is the cage. The cage needs somewhere to stand, which is the tripod. The tripod makes the light worth aiming. Nothing sits in a drawer waiting for the rest of the kit to arrive.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a beginner content creator buy first?

A wireless lavalier microphone. Audio is the fault an audience reacts to fastest — viewers will watch imperfect footage with clean sound and leave clean footage with echoey sound. A phone's built-in microphone sits an arm's length from your mouth and records the room; a lavalier moves it to your collar, which is the single largest improvement available for the money.

Do I need a cage before a microphone?

No. The cage is second, not first. It makes everything else easier to mount and steadies your framing, but a viewer forgives a slightly unsteady shot far more readily than they forgive bad sound. Buy the microphone, then the cage that will hold it.

Is a tripod really more important than a light?

It depends on whether you appear in your own videos. If you film yourself alone, a tripod is what makes the shot possible at all, which outranks making an existing shot look better. If someone else holds the camera, move the light ahead of it.

What is the fifth purchase, and does everyone need it?

It splits by where you film. If you shoot outdoors, buy an ND filter — it is what lets you hold a cinematic shutter angle in daylight. If you shoot indoors and have started recording in high-bitrate formats, buy fast storage instead. Most creators need one of the two, not both.

Where does a gimbal fit in this ranking?

Sixth at the earliest, and for many creators never. It costs more than the first three purchases combined and solves walking shots, which is not what most new creators are filming. Buy it when handheld movement is genuinely limiting a shot you want to make.
